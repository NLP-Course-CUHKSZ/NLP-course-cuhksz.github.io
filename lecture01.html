4What's new in 2026

Even an introduction moves with the field. Six trends to watch, each tied to a slide of this year's lecture:

🔧

From models to harnesses

The course now has a lecture on LLM agents and harness engineering: building the system of tools, checks and feedback loops around a model (slide 16).

OpenAI, Harness engineering: leveraging Codex in an agent-first world (Feb 2026)
⏱

Measuring AI by task length

Slide 118 asks how long a task AI can finish. METR measured it: the length of tasks frontier models complete with 50% success has doubled about every seven months since 2019 (slide 118).

Kwa, West et al., Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Tasks (METR, Mar 2025)
📚

Tens of trillions of tokens

A 13-year-old has heard fewer than 100 million words; Qwen3 was pre-trained on 36 trillion tokens in 119 languages and dialects (slide 87).

Qwen3 Technical Report (May 2025)
🔄

Recursive self-improvement, in the open

AI that helps build better AI is the group's research direction (slide 111), and in 2026 it got a dedicated workshop at ICLR.

ICLR 2026 Workshop on AI with Recursive Self-Improvement (Apr 2026, Rio de Janeiro)
🛡

Oversight must scale too

As capability grows, human supervision stays flat. The open problem is oversight where AI helps generate feedback and monitor, so supervision grows with the model (slide 114).

The lecture, Chapter 9
🏆

Projects that ship

New this year, a final project can be a competition entry with a technical report. Strong proposals may get compute for a 10B+ model, and the goal is 100+ GitHub stars (slide 31, slide 32).

The lecture, Chapter 3

5Quotes & reading

"You shall know a word by the company it keeps!"

J. R. Firth, A Synopsis of Linguistic Theory, 1930–1955 (1957)
The distributional hypothesis behind word2vec and GPT (slide 76), and Assignment 1.

"The fundamental problem of communication is that of reproducing at one point either exactly or approximately a message selected at another point."

Claude Shannon, A Mathematical Theory of Communication (1948)
The 1948 paper where n-gram models of English begin, the first entry on the timeline of Chapter 7.

"I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'"

Alan Turing, Computing Machinery and Intelligence, Mind (1950)
Turing's test is a conversation in natural language; the surveys in Chapter 9 ask what machines can do today.

"The biggest lesson that can be read from 70 years of AI research is that general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin."

Rich Sutton, The Bitter Lesson (2019)
Why scalable objectives and compute (Chapter 7) replaced hand-built pipelines (Chapter 6).
