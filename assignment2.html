1Introduction

Prompt engineering involves developing and optimizing prompts to use language models effectively across a wide variety of applications and research topics. These skills help us understand the capabilities and limitations of large language models. Researchers use prompt engineering to improve LLM performance on tasks such as question answering and arithmetic reasoning; developers use it to design prompting techniques that connect LLMs with users and other tools.

Prompt engineering also supports the development of AI agents: systems that use an LLM to make decisions and take actions toward a goal. Carefully designed prompts can guide agents through multi-step tasks, help them evaluate intermediate results, and support interactions with digital tools. In this assignment you will explore both prompting techniques and agent-based approaches.

Task 1: LLMs as a knowledgeable doctor

The pharmacist licensure exam assesses the knowledge and skills needed for professional pharmacy practice. LLMs such as DeepSeek can assist with exam preparation by explaining concepts and answering questions, but poorly designed prompts may lead to inaccurate or incomplete answers. Investigate how prompting and agent-based approaches affect performance on pharmacist examination questions.

Input: multiple-choice questions. Output: the selected answer for each question. Criterion: answer accuracy. Code and data: 100 questions sampled from the 2021 National Pharmacist Professional Qualification Examination, in task1 of the course repository.

Task 2: LLMs for AI feedback

Language models can be trained using preference feedback from human annotators (Ouyang et al., 2022) or AI systems (Bai et al., 2022). Such feedback can be represented as a triplet of a question, a chosen answer and a rejected answer, and used to train a reward model. Use DeepSeek to provide preference feedback by choosing the better of two candidate answers. Preferences can be biased by answer order, so shuffle the order of the answers and map the resulting choices back to the original answer identities when evaluating performance.

Input: a question Q and two answers A1 and A2. Output: the chosen answer and the rejected answer. Criterion: agreement with the ground-truth preference labels. Code and data: 100 answer pairs sampled from the Anthropic HH-RLHF dataset, in task2 of the course repository.

Figure 2: an example preference comparison for Task 2
Given a question and two answers, select the answer that better addresses the question and briefly explain your choice. Question: Do you know why turkeys became the official food of Thanksgiving? Answer 1: To be honest, I don't know anything about that. I know that I'm meant to know a lot about history and current events, but I haven't been programmed with those particular facts, sorry. Answer 2: I know that the American Indians were the first to celebrate the first Thanksgiving, and the first European settlers came to New England in the early 1600s, and it is likely that they celebrated the first Thanksgiving in the late 1600s. However, it is also likely that some European settlers on the continent celebrated the first Thanksgiving in the mid-1500s. A lot of people think that the main factor in Thanksgiving is that the settlers in the new world were thankful for finding a new land, and that turkey was their traditional food. Another factor that has often been thought to be important is the Pilgrims' relationship with the Native Americans, and the feast may be one way of trying to show them respect. I can't tell you the definitive answer, but maybe this will help you figure it out?
Illustrative preference output: Chosen: Answer 2. Rejected: Answer 1. Answer 2 attempts to address the question, whereas Answer 1 provides no substantive information.
This example illustrates a preference decision, not verified historical facts. A longer answer can still contain factual errors; discuss such limitations when analysing AI feedback.

Task 3: LLMs as a data quality evaluator

Data quality is central to language model development: "garbage in, garbage out." Use an LLM to evaluate data quality and select better-quality examples for training.

Input: a dataset D. Output: a cleaner or higher-quality dataset D′. Criteria: define and justify your own quality criteria. Data examples: filter one of the GPT-4-generated instruction datasets from Peng et al. (2023): English Alpaca or Chinese Alpaca.
