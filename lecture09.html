Lecture 9: Medical LLMs — Towards Generalist Medical AI

What happens when everything from pre-training to agents meets medicine, where the data are private, the stakes are high and “good on the benchmark” is not good enough? Follow the story of HuatuoGPT, the road to generalist medical AI, and why the next leap may come from environments. 1The lecture map

How do we get from a chatbot that talks like a doctor to a generalist medical AI that people can actually trust?

Five acts, eleven chapters. First why medicine is worth it, then the HuatuoGPT story of data, evaluation and knowledge, then the capabilities a generalist needs (perception, reasoning, generation, languages) and what blocks them, then the claim that the next leap comes from environments — simulated patients, hospitals and cells — and finally what is deployed today and where it goes next. The thesis (slide 3): generalist medical AI is the goal, trustworthy medical AI is the way, environments are the engine. Click a chapter to watch it.

    Why Medical AI? Domains, Giants and Timing

    A focused domain is a cheaper sandbox for the big AGI questions. Medicine helps everyone, is hard enough, attracts funding and serves the public good — and with aging populations, stronger models and real adoption, the gap is no longer exam scores but trust and workflow.

    → So how do you actually build a model that behaves like a doctor?

    The Story of HuatuoGPT: Data, Hybrid SFT and RLMF

    Start from Huatuo-26M, about 26 million medical QA pairs, most of them real online consultations. Mix doctors' answers polished by an LLM with LLM answers pushed to act like doctors, then run RL where ChatGPT judges but a real doctor's answer anchors every judgment.

2Try it in Colab

Three short notebooks turn the lecture into experiments with a small open model (Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct) and public medical datasets. Click Open in Colab, then Runtime → Run all. Each ends with exercises. All three run on a free CPU runtime; a T4 GPU (Runtime → Change runtime type) makes them faster and lets you try bigger models. These notebooks are about evaluating models. Nothing a model writes in them is medical advice.

Notebook 1 · Chapters 3 & 6 · ~10 min

Evaluate a small model on medical exam questions

  • Score real MedQA (USMLE-style) questions from letter probabilities
  • Shuffle the options and watch the score move (position bias)
  • Check whether confidence predicts correctness
  • Remove the options and see why open answers need a verifier
.ipynb · CPU or GPU
Notebook 2 · Chapters 8–9 · ~10 min

Retrieval-augmented answering with citations

  • Build an evidence base from 1,000 PubMed abstracts (PubMedQA)
  • Write a BM25 retriever and measure recall@k
  • Compare closed-book answers with retrieved and oracle evidence
  • Check citations automatically, and abstain when no evidence is found
.ipynb · CPU or GPU
Notebook 3 · Chapters 2–3 · ~5 min

An LLM judge: position bias and order swapping

  • Judge pairs of answers with a small LLM, in both orders
  • Measure how often the first answer wins regardless of content
  • See the order-agreement check (as in HuatuoGPT's reward model) flag a biased judge, and averaging both orders recover the signal
  • Anchor the judge with a doctor's reference answer (RLMF)
.ipynb · CPU or GPU

4What's new in 2026

The lecture's own thesis is that the next gains come from environments, rubrics and trust rather than from more text (slide 167). Six things that changed recently:

📋

Rubrics became rewards

Physician-written rubrics, first used to grade answers, now also train models. In the lecture, an 8B HuatuoGPT-III adapted to medicine with RL only (no SFT stage) scores 67.5 on HealthBench, above the proprietary models listed — while frontier models stay ahead on hard exam questions (slide 147).

HealthBench: 5,000 conversations, rubrics by 262 physicians (OpenAI, arXiv 2505.08775) · slides 146–147
🔄

Benchmarks that stay fresh

To beat saturation and leakage, benchmarks keep adding new cases and follow one patient over several stages and images, and experts write questions until every model fails them (slide 140, slide 142).

LiveClin (ICLR 2026) · Dentists' Last Exam · slides 140–142
🏥

From datasets to environments

Simulated patients, clinical world models and digital hospitals let agents act, get outcome feedback and improve — in medicine, the “era of experience” argument (slide 109, slide 111).

Silver & Sutton, Welcome to the Era of Experience (2025) · slides 109–119
🧰

Medical skills for general agents

Expertise is packaged as skills a general agent loads on demand, and the agent runs on a desktop box inside the hospital, so patient data stay in the building (slide 156, slide 157).

OpenClaw-Medical-Skills (~869 skills at the time of the slide) · OpenClaw-Med at CUHK-Shenzhen Hospital · slides 156–158
🧬

Virtual cells go zero-shot

Arc Institute's first Virtual Cell Challenge (2025) drew more than 5,000 participants from 114 countries; the 2026 edition asks for perturbation responses in cell lines the model has never seen. Beating simple baselines is still hard (slide 129).

2025 wrap-up · 2026 challenge (Arc Institute)
📚

Evidence at the point of care

Evidence-grounded assistants are part of daily practice: the lecture cites OpenEvidence's report that about 65% of US physicians use it. The open question is who maintains the evidence — the lecture proposes an open, community-edited “AI-native UpToDate” (slide 132, slide 134).

OpenEvidence figure: company figures reported by NBC News (May 2026), as cited on slide 132

5Quotes & reading

"We propose a new paradigm for medical AI, which we refer to as generalist medical AI (GMAI)."

Moor et al., Foundation models for generalist medical artificial intelligence, Nature (2023)
The goal named in the lecture's title (slide 47).

"Evidence based medicine is the conscientious, explicit, and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about the care of individual patients."

David Sackett et al., Evidence based medicine: what it is and what it isn't, BMJ (1996)
What “evidence-grounded” medical AI has to live up to (Chapter 9).

"Pre-training as we know it will unquestionably end."

Ilya Sutskever, NeurIPS 2024 Test of Time talk, as reported by The Verge and Reuters; shown on slide 79
Why adding more medical text stopped being enough, and reasoning came next.

"A new generation of agents will acquire superhuman capabilities by learning predominantly from experience."

David Silver & Richard Sutton, Welcome to the Era of Experience (2025)
The case for environments over datasets (Chapter 8).
